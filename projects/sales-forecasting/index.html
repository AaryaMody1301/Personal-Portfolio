02 / The problem

Start with the right question.

A convincing forecast chart can hide target imputation, leakage, or an unfair baseline comparison. Model complexity does not establish that a forecast improves on the last observed value.

03 / My contribution

What I built.

I developed the package and CLI around explicit dataset contracts, expanding-window backtests, chronological model tuning, validation-only ensemble weighting, and manifest-backed dashboard outputs.

04 / The approach

Make each step inspectable.

  1. 01

    Validate observed data

    Check timestamps and targets. The supported workflow does not silently impute missing targets.

  2. 02

    Respect time

    Use expanding-window evaluation and nested chronological tuning. Expose genuinely known-future regressors only through the requested horizon.

  3. 03

    Compare reproducibly

    Evaluate models against the same baseline and folds, fingerprint the run, and verify saved artifacts before the dashboard opens them.

05 / Evidence

What supports the story.

  • The reviewed benchmark target is weekly median vehicle selling price, rather than sales volume.
  • On the published two-fold benchmark, mean fold RMSE is 1764.63 for ARIMA, 1783.29 for ETS, and 1891.95 for the last-value baseline.
  • The reported RMSE is the arithmetic mean of fold RMSE values, not a pooled error over concatenated predictions.

06 / Scope & limitations

Keep the boundaries visible.

The reviewed segment contains 32 observed weeks from December 2014 to July 2015, with 24 initial training observations and two four-week holdout folds. This is a small reproducibility/acceptance benchmark, not evidence that one model is universally best. LSTM is not part of the supported release.

Start with a fair baseline. Let the evaluation justify the extra complexity.
