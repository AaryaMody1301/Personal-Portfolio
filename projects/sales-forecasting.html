The problem

A forecast can look persuasive when future information leaks into training or a complex model is never compared with a simple baseline. I built an evaluation pipeline that makes those choices explicit and preserves the evidence needed to reproduce the result.

The release benchmark forecasts weekly median vehicle selling price. The original repository is named Sales Forecasting; its release benchmark targets vehicle price, rather than revenue or units sold.

The approach

  1. Preserve observed targets. Parse and validate source timestamps and prices; do not fill missing target periods.
  2. Split chronologically. Use expanding-window backtests with a 24-week initial training period and two four-week outer holdouts.
  3. Keep tuning inside training. Tune machine-learning models on nested chronological splits; learn ensemble weights from validation data.
  4. Ship reproducible artifacts. Record the environment, checksum metadata, leaderboard, and numerical acceptance contract.

The reviewed series contains 32 contiguous weekly observations from 21 December 2014 to 26 July 2015. The source has longer gaps; this acceptance benchmark uses the longest contiguous observed block.

Decisions & limits

Only ARIMA and ETS beat the last-value baseline in this acceptance benchmark. The validation-weighted ensemble was 37.46% worse by the ranking metric. I keep that result visible because a failed comparison still informs a useful engineering decision.

With 32 observations and two outer folds, these results are too narrow to establish model superiority. The benchmark is a reproducibility and release-acceptance check, not a broad performance study.

I did not add an LSTM to the supported release. A future neural model needs a substantially larger real series and useful out-of-sample improvement under the same chronological evaluation rules.
